Robust anion conductive polymers containing perfluoroalkylene and pendant ammonium groups for high performance fuel cells†

Abstract

A novel series of ammonium-containing copolymers (QPAF-4) were designed and synthesized as anion exchange membranes for alkaline fuel cell applications. The copolymers were prepared via a nickel promoted polycondensation reaction with high molecular weights (Mw = 72.7–276.4 kDa as precursors) and were composed of perfluoroalkylene and fluorenyl groups with pendant ammonium groups. The QPAF-4 membrane with optimized copolymer composition and ion exchange capacity exhibited high hydroxide ion conductivity (86.2 mS cm−1 in water at 80 °C) and excellent mechanical properties (large elongation at break = 269%). A severe alkaline stability test of the QPAF-4 membranes in 1 M KOH at 80 °C for 1000 h and the post-test analyses of the 1H NMR spectra, solubility, and mechanical properties revealed minor, or no, changes in the chemical structure and properties. Alkaline fuel cells using the QPAF-4 membrane were operated using hydrazine as a fuel and oxygen or air as oxidant to achieve the high maximum power density of 515 mW cm−2. The durability of the membrane was also confirmed in the operating fuel cell at a constant current density for longer than 1000 h.

1. Introduction

Fuel cells are promising energy conversion system alternatives to internal combustion engines because of their high efficiency and low pollution levels. Among several types of fuel cell, polymer electrolyte membrane fuel cells are suitable for applications for electric vehicles, portable devices, and residential power supplies. Anion exchange membrane fuel cells (AEMFCs) provide advantages over proton exchange membrane fuel cells including the possible use of nonprecious metals as electrocatalysts and the fast kinetics of the oxygen reduction reaction under alkaline conditions. Although a number of technical efforts have been made to develop anion exchange membranes, the existing AEMs are not as conductive and stable as proton exchange membranes.1 The critical issues are mainly related to the insufficient alkaline stability of the quaternary ammonium groups as ion exchange sites.2 While there have been a large number of studies on the alkaline stability of onium groups such as imidazoliums,3 guanidiniums,4 sulfoniums,5 and phosphoniums,6 still the most used ones are tetraalkylammonium groups due to their balanced properties of stability, anion conductivity, and synthetic freedom. The major mechanisms of the degradation of the ammonium groups by hydroxide ions include Hofmann (β hydrogen) elimination, nucleophilic substitution (SN2), Stevens or Sommelet–Hauser rearrangement, and ylide formation.7 Even when β hydrogen atoms are not present, the ammonium groups attached to the polymers via benzylic carbons are known to be vulnerable to nucleophilic substitution reactions.8

Several groups have independently proposed either theoretically or experimentally that trimethylammonium groups spaced by alkylene groups from the polymer main chains are more stable than the trimethylbenzylammonium group under alkaline conditions, although they contain β hydrogen atoms and could potentially experience Hofmann elimination. A DFT study on substituted trimethylammonium cations by Long et al. suggested that the reaction free energy barrier for the Hofmann elimination significantly increased as the number of carbon atoms in the alkylene spacer chain increased from 2 (ca. 17.5 kcal mol−1) to 6 (ca. 24.0 kcal mol−1).9 Lee et al. reported that fluorene-based aromatic copolymers tethered with pendant trimethylammonium groups (spaced by hexylene groups between the fluorene and the ammonium groups) exhibited high alkaline stability in 1 M KOH at 80 °C for 720 h with no practical changes in the chemical structure and ion exchange capacity (IEC). However, the high IEC (and accordingly the highly anion conductive) membrane lost mechanical strength and became fragile after the stability test for 168 h, possibly because of the morphological change based on the self-aggregation of the ammonium groups.10 Contradictory results were obtained by Parrondo et al. who claimed that trimethylammonium groups with hexyl spacers degraded faster than those attached to benzyl groups under alkaline conditions when poly(phenylene oxide) was used as the polymer main chain.11 Thus, the effect of the spacer groups on the stability of the ammonium groups still seems to be controversial and needs further investigation in relation to the polymer main chain structure.

Recently, we reported that the absence of heteroatom linkages (e.g., ether and sulfone) in the main chain contributed much to improving the alkaline stability of AEMs. In particular, introducing perfluoroalkylene groups provided the ammonium-functionalized aromatic copolymers (QPAF-1, Fig. 1) with good membrane forming capability and flexibility.12 Other advantages of introducing perfluoroalkylene groups into the polymer main chains include excellent solvent solubility (soluble in common solvents such as methanol and ethanol) and membrane flexibility (similar elasticity to Nafion membrane and state-of-the-art proton conductive membranes). The trimethylbenzylammonium groups, however, exhibited insufficient alkaline stability and degraded within several hundred hours in 1 M KOH at 80 °C.


Fig. 1 The chemical structure of QPAF-1.

The objective of the present research is to combine the partially fluorinated alkylene main chains with fluorenyl groups containing pendant ammoniums to produce chemically and physically robust anion conductive polymer membranes (QPAF-4) with high anion conductivity and bendability. Unlike the typical aromatic polymer-based AEMs, the thin QPAF-4 membranes obtained were bendable and were able to be folded into an origami bird. The membranes did not show evidence of structural and property changes after a severe alkaline stability test in 1 M KOH at 80 °C for 1000 h. The properties and fuel cell performance of the QPAF-4 membranes were investigated in detail to evaluate the effectiveness of our molecular strategy to produce better performing AEMs.

2. Experimental section

2.1. Polymerization

Monomers 1 and 2 were synthesized according to the literature (see ESI†).12–15 A typical procedure is as follows.16 A 100 mL three-necked flask equipped with a nitrogen inlet and mechanical stirrer was charged with monomer 1 (1.52 g, 2.91 mmol), monomer 2 (0.82 g, 1.67 mmol), 2,2′-bipyridine (1.70 g, 10.91 mmol), and DMAc (11 mL). The mixture was heated at 80 °C to obtain a homogeneous mixture. To the mixture, Ni(COD)2 (3.00 g, 10.9 mmol) was added. After polymerization for 3 h, the mixture was poured into a mixture of methanol and concentrated hydrochloric acid (1:1 by volume, 150 mL) to precipitate a black fibrous solid. The crude product was washed with concentrated hydrochloric acid (100 mL) and then treated with 0.2 M potassium carbonate aqueous solution (200 mL). The pale yellow product was washed with deionized water (200 mL) and dried at 60 °C in a vacuum oven to obtain 1.92 g of PAF-4 (m1.00 n0.57) as a yellow fibrous solid in 96% yield.

2.2. Preparation of QPAF-4 membranes

A typical procedure is as follows. A 50 mL round-bottomed flask was charged with PAF-4 (m1.00 n0.57) (1.70 g, 2.88 mmol of dimethylamino groups) and DMAc (9.6 mL). After dissolution of the polymer, iodomethane (0.45 mL, 7.22 mmol) was added into the mixture. The mixture was stirred for 24 h at r.t. and diluted with DMAc (10 mL). The mixture was filtered with a syringe stuffed with cotton and the filtrate was cast onto a flat glass plate. Drying the solution at 50 °C overnight produced a transparent light brown membrane. The resulting membrane was washed with deionized water (2 L) and dried at 60 °C in a vacuum oven to obtain the QPAF-4 membrane (ca. 50 μm thick). The counter anions were converted to hydroxide ions by soaking the membrane in a 1 M KOH aqueous solution at 80 °C for 48 h. The resulting membrane was washed and immersed in degassed, deionized water for at least 1 day to ensure complete removal of excess KOH. QPAF-4 in the chloride ion form was prepared by treating the membrane in the hydroxide ion form with 1 M hydrochloric acid for 48 h at r.t.

2.3. Preparation of catalyst coated membrane (CCM)

1.0 g of Ni/C (50 wt% Ni on ECP600JD) powder was mixed with 5.5 g of 2.0 wt% QPAF-4 (IEC = 1.11 meq. g−1), and 23.5 g of DMF/EtOH solution (1:1 by weight). The mixture was mixed using a ball mill with zirconia beads (diameter = 5.0 mm, Nikkato Corporation) for 50 min at 240 rpm. The obtained slurry was sprayed onto one side of the QPAF-4 membrane (IEC = 1.11 meq. g−1 and thickness = 50 μm) to form the anode catalyst layer. The cathode catalyst layer was formed on the other side of the membrane in a similar manner with the slurry prepared from 1.0 g of Pt/C (TEC10E50E, Tanaka Kikinzoku Kogyo) powder and 15.0 g of 2.0 wt% QPAF-4 (IEC = 2.07 meq. g−1) solution in DMF/EtOH. The loaded amounts of the catalysts were ca. 2.6 mg cm−2 for Ni/C in the anode and ca. 1.25 mg cm−2 for Pt/C in the cathode. The obtained CCM was pressed at 3.25 MPa for 30 s at room temperature to ensure tight contact between the catalyst layers and the membrane.

2.4. Fuel cell operation

The CCM with 4 cm2 of the geometric electrode area was mounted in a single cell and treated with 1.0 M KOH aqueous solution for 72 h at 60 °C to ensure the ion exchange reaction. A fuel cell was operated at 60 °C, supplying a mixture of 1.0 M KOH and 10 wt% hydrazine aqueous solution to the anode at a flow rate of 1.2 mL min−1 and humidified oxygen or air (62% relative humidity) to the cathode at a flow rate of 0.1 mL min−1. The operating pressure was set at ambient pressure for the anode and 10 kPa for the cathode. Serpentine and comb-shaped flow fields were used for the anode and the cathode, respectively.

3. Results and discussion

3.1. Synthesis of QPAF-4 copolymers and membranes

The synthetic procedure of the QPAF-4 copolymers is shown in Scheme 1. Precursor copolymers, PAF-4, were synthesized by a Ni-promoted polycondensation reaction of monomers 1 and 2. The series of copolymers, PAF-4 (1–4), with different copolymer compositions (m and n) were obtained with high molecular weights (Mn = 7.1–16.9 kDa and Mw = 72.7–276.4 kDa) (Table 1). The polydispersity index (Mw/Mn = 10.2–17.4) was relatively high for this kind of polycondensation reaction, probably due to the inefficient polymerization reaction. The obtained PAF-4 were soluble in some organic solvents such as TCE, chloroform and DMAc. The chemical structures of the PAF-4 were analyzed by 1H NMR spectroscopy and a typical spectrum example is shown in Fig. 2a. All of the peaks were well-assigned to the supposed chemical structure. The copolymer compositions estimated from the integral peak ratios between 1–7 and 10, 11 were in good accordance with those of the feed comonomer ratios, indicating successful and quantitative copolycondensation reactions. A quaternization reaction of PAF-4 was conducted using iodomethane in DMAc solution and direct casting from the reaction mixture provided yellow, transparent, and tough QPAF-4 membranes in iodide ion forms. Compared to a number of ion-containing polymer membranes that we have previously developed, the QPAF-4 membranes were more flexible and bendable and even foldable. An origami bird could be folded from the QPAF-4 membranes (Fig. S2 in ESI†). The QPAF-4 were soluble in DMAc and DMSO. The solubility of the QPAF-4 in methanol and acetone changed with their ion exchange capacity (IEC); QPAF-4 with IECs lower than 1.02 meq. g−1 were soluble in acetone and those with IECs higher than 1.47 meq. g−1 were soluble in methanol. The chemical structure of the QPAF-4 in the chloride ion form was analyzed from the 1H NMR spectra (Fig. 2b), in which a quaternization reaction was confirmed by the shift of the methylene and methyl protons attached to the nitrogen atoms to the lower magnetic field compared with those of the precursor PAF-4. The integral peak ratios between 1–7 and 14 revealed the complete quaternization. The IEC values were estimated by titration, and were in good accordance with those calculated from the copolymer compositions (obtained from the 1H NMR spectra) of QPAF-4 within acceptable errors (Table 1). We hereafter refer to the titrated IEC values.

3.2. Morphology

Fig. 3 shows a cross-sectional TEM image of a QPAF-4 membrane (IEC = 1.47 meq. g−1) stained with tetrachloroplatinate ions. The dark areas represent hydrophilic domains composed of stained ammonium groups and their aggregates, while the bright areas represent hydrophobic domains. Both the hydrophilic and hydrophobic domains are spherical, ca. 1.5 nm in diameter, and homogeneous throughout. The morphology is similar to that of our previous quaternized copolymer membrane, QPAF-1 (IEC = 1.26 meq. g−1), sharing the same hydrophobic components but different hydrophilic groups.12 The results suggest that the phase-separated morphology was more dependent on the strongly hydrophobic perfluoroalkylene groups than the hydrophilic components with ammonium groups either on the main chains or on the side chains.
Fig. 3 A TEM image of a QPAF-4 membrane (IEC = 1.47 meq. g−1) stained with tetrachloroplatinate ions.

The morphological changes with humidity were investigated using small-angle X-ray scattering (SAXS). The SAXS profiles of the QPAF-4 (1.47 meq. g−1) membrane in the chloride ion form were collected at 30–90% relative humidity (RH) at 40 °C and compared with those of the QPAF-1 (1.26 meq. g−1) membrane in Fig. 4. For QPAF-1, a broad peak was observed at ca. 1 nm−1 of the scattering vector (q) or 6 nm of the d spacing at 30% RH, which developed slightly as the humidity increased. The d value was ca. 7 nm at 90% RH. It is assumed that the d spacing is related to the distance between the hydrophilic domains. The QPAF-4 membrane exhibited similar SAXS curves and humidity dependence. A more prominent peak appeared at q = ca. 0.7 nm−1 or d = ca. 8 nm at 30% RH, which developed more as the humidity increased. The d value was ca. 9 nm at 90% RH. From the development and the shift of the peaks with the humidity, it can be reasonably assumed that the d spacing is related to the distance between the hydrophilic domains, which is similar to the QPAF-1 membrane. The more pronounced peak development for the QPAF-4 membrane compared with that for the QPAF-1 membrane reflects the former’s larger water absorbability (see Fig. 5 and the below discussion).

Fig. 5 (a) The water uptake at room temperature and the (b) OH− conductivity at 30 °C of the QPAF-4 and QPAF-1 membranes as a function of the IEC.

3.3. Water uptake and ion conductivity

The water uptake and OH− conductivity of the QPAF-4 membranes are shown with those of the QPAF-1 membranes in Fig. 5. The QPAF-4 membranes exhibited somewhat higher water uptakes than those of the QPAF-1 membranes at any IEC investigated. The pendant ammonium groups are likely to absorb more water than the trimethylbenzylammonium groups due to the larger free volume produced by the C6 alkyl spacers. Similar results were reported in the literature that the water uptake increased as the side chain length increased from C1 to C5 or C7 for poly(phenylene oxide).17 The QPAF-1 and QPAF-4 membranes exhibited similar OH− conductivity and IEC dependence. It is noticeable that both of the membranes showed a decrease in the conductivity as the IEC increased above 1.5 meq. g−1. The decrease in the conductivity is presumably related to the high water absorbability of the high IEC membranes causing lower effective IEC values, taking water molecules into account. For example, the gravimetric and volumetric IECs of the QPAF-4 (1.84 meq. g−1) membrane are 0.65 meq. g−1 and 0.66 meq. cm−3, respectively, in the fully swollen state taking the water uptake and dimensional change into account. Among the four QPAF-4 membranes, the QPAF-4 with IEC = 1.47 meq. g−1 showed the most balanced properties with the highest ion conductivity (47.8 mS cm−1) and reasonable water uptake (105%).

The temperature dependence of the OH− conductivity of the QPAF-4 and QPAF-1 membranes in water is shown in Fig. 6. Both of the types of membrane showed approximate Arrhenius-type temperature dependence up to 80 °C. The QPAF-4 membrane with IEC = 1.47 meq. g−1 achieved the highest conductivity of 86.2 mS cm−1 at 80 °C. The apparent activation energies were estimated from the slopes to be 9.8–11.3 kJ mol−1, which were similar to those of the QPAF-1 (11.7 kJ mol−1) and other aromatic polymer-based AEMs reported in the literature.18–21 The results indicate that the position of the ammonium groups, or the alkyl spacer length, is unlikely to affect the ion-conduction mechanism.


Fig. 6 The temperature dependence of the OH− conductivity of the QPAF-4 and QPAF-1 membranes in water.

3.4. Gas permeability

The hydrogen and oxygen gas permeabilities of the QPAF-4 membranes in the chloride ion forms were measured at 80 °C and plotted as functions of the humidity, as shown in Fig. 7. Among the four QPAF-4 membranes, the lowest IEC membrane (IEC = 0.75 meq. g−1) exhibited the lowest hydrogen (2.2 × 10−9 cm3 (STP) cm s−1 cm−2 cmHg−1) and oxygen (0.5 × 10−9 cm3 (STP) cm s−1 cm−2 cmHg−1) permeabilities. It is assumed that the lower free volume (due to the lower content of pendant ammonium groups) caused lower gas permeability. This assumption is not contradictory to the results that QPAF-1 with benzylammonium groups demonstrated a lower gas permeability than the QPAF-4 membrane. Compared to Nafion (IEC = 0.91 meq. g−1) composed of fully perfluorinated main chain and side chain structures, the QPAF-4 membrane with a comparable IEC (1.02 meq. g−1) showed lower hydrogen and oxygen permeabilities. Rigid fluorenylbiphenylene groups composed of four phenylene rings in QPAF-4 would have interpolymer interactions, at least more than in Nafion, that are accountable for the lower gas permeabilities.
Fig. 7 The hydrogen (circle) and oxygen (triangle) gas permeability of Nafion (in the H+ form, dashed lines) and the QPAF-4 and QPAF-1 membranes (in the Cl− forms, solid lines) at 80 °C as a function of the humidity.

3.5. Mechanical properties

As a mechanical stability test, dynamic mechanical analysis (DMA) was carried out for the QPAF-4 membranes in the chloride ion forms. The storage moduli (E′), loss moduli (E′′), and tanδ (= E′′/E′) are shown as functions of the relative humidity at 80 °C in Fig. 8a and as functions of the temperature at 60% RH in Fig. 8b. Data for QPAF-1 in the chloride ion form and Nafion in the H+ form are also included. In the humidity dependence curves, the E′ and E′′ of the QPAF-4 membranes decreased as the IEC values decreased. It is thought that the presence of perfluoroalkylene groups lowered the storage and loss moduli, similar to the cases for the QPAF-1 membranes. In addition, since E′ and E′′ decreased as the humidity increased, water molecules presumably functioned as a plasticizer. The changes in the viscoelastic properties of the QPAF-4 membranes were more significant in the temperature dependence curves. The lower IEC membranes exhibited a loss in the E′ and a peak in the E′′ and tanδ at lower temperatures. These thermal behaviors would be associated with the glass transition temperature of QPAF-4 most likely to be based on the micro-Brownian motion of the perfluoroalkylene groups. Similar results were obtained for the QPAF-1 membranes sharing the same perfluoroalkylene groups.
Fig. 8 DMA curves of the QPAF-4 and QPAF-1 membranes (in the Cl− forms, solid lines) and Nafion (in the H+ form, dashed lines): the (a) humidity dependence at 80 °C and (b) temperature dependence at 60% RH.

Elongation tests were then carried out for the QPAF-4 membranes at 80 °C and 60% RH (Fig. 9). The QPAF-4 membrane with the lowest IEC value (0.75 meq. g−1), whose perfluoroalkyl content was the largest, exhibited the highest elongation at break (414%), comparable to that of Nafion (404%). The highest maximum stress (22.7 MPa) was achieved with the QPAF-4 with IEC = 1.47 meq. g−1 membrane. The stretching properties of QPAF-4 were similar to those of QPAF-1, taking into account some differences in their IECs and molecular weights (compare QPAF-4 (IEC = 1.02 meq. g−1, Mn = 7.1 kDa, and Mw = 72.7 kDa) and QPAF-1 (IEC = 1.26 meq. g−1, Mn = 9.1 kDa, and Mw = 49.0 kDa)).


Fig. 9 Stress versus strain curves of the QPAF-4 and QPAF-1 membranes (in the Cl− forms, solid lines) and Nafion (in the H+ form, dashed line) at 80 °C and 60% RH.

3.6. Alkaline and oxidative stabilities

The alkaline stability of the QPAF-4 membranes was tested in 1 M KOH at 80 °C (Fig. 10). The four QPAF-4 membranes with different IEC values exhibited excellent stability, retaining their initial hydroxide ion conductivities after 1000 h (slight increases in the conductivity were within acceptable errors). In contrast, the QPAF-1 membrane exhibited a gradual decrease in hydroxide ion conductivity from 43.0 mS cm−1 to 2.3 mS cm−1 after 1000 h because of the degradation of the trimethylbenzylammonium groups. The results indicate that the trimethylammonium groups were much more stable when at the terminal of the side chains rather than on the benzyl groups in the main chain. In the literature there are some anion conductive membranes which are durable under similar alkaline conditions.22,23 However, most experience some chemical changes accompanied by losses in solubility and mechanical strength. The post-test QPAF-4 membranes did not undergo changes in solubility, and remained soluble in DMSO and DMAc. In addition, the post-test membranes retained their shape and bendability (Fig. S3 in ESI†).
Fig. 10 The time course of the hydroxide ion conductivity of the QPAF-4 and -1 membranes in 1 M KOH aqueous solution at 80 °C.

In order to further confirm their alkaline stability, the post-test QPAF-4 membranes were subjected to stress/strain measurements (Fig. 11) and NMR analyses (Fig. 12). In the 1H NMR spectra of the QPAF-4 (IEC = 1.47 meq. g−1), the peak positions and their integrals were the same between the pristine and the post-test membranes; the calculated IEC values were 1.62 meq. g−1 for both of the samples. No additional peaks were detected in the spectrum of the post-test sample. The S–S curves showed only minor changes with acceptable errors in the maximum stress and yield strain between the pristine and post-test membranes. These results are solid evidence of the chemical robustness of the QPAF-4 membranes both in the polymer main chains and the pendant ammonium groups under the severe alkaline stability test for 1000 h. Such high alkaline stability would be attributed to the lack of heteroatom linkages in the main chain and the presence of aliphatic (C6) spacers between the ammonium groups and the main chain. To the best of our knowledge, the alkaline stability of the QPAF-4 membranes is among the best reported in the literature.24


Fig. 11 Stress versus strain curves of the QPAF-4 membranes in the Cl− forms before (dashed lines) and after (solid lines) the alkaline stability test at 80 °C and 60% RH.

Fig. 12 1H NMR spectra of the QPAF-4 (IEC = 1.47 meq. g−1 in the Cl− form) before (black line) and after (blue line) the alkaline stability test in DMSO-d6.

The oxidative stability of the QPAF-4 membrane (IEC = 1.47 meq. g−1) was then investigated in Fenton’s solution (3% H2O2 containing 2 ppm FeSO4) at 80 °C. After 8 h, the membrane was still self-standing and bendable, but lost transparency and became turbid (Fig. S4a in ESI†). Since the post-test membrane was soluble in DMSO, the 1H NMR spectrum was recorded (Fig. S4b in ESI†). Compared to those of the pristine membrane, the peak positions and their integral ratios did not change indicating no detectable chemical structural decompositions. The post-test membrane exhibited a hydroxide ion conductivity of 43 mS cm−1 in water at 30 °C. The minor decrease in the conductivity compared to the initial value (47.8 mS cm−1) might be indicative of morphological changes of the membrane associated with the loss of transparency.

3.7. Fuel cell performance

A catalyst-coated membrane was prepared with the QPAF-4 membrane (IEC = 2.07 meq. g−1, 50 μm thick, and not listed in Table 1 but newly prepared for the fuel cell experiment with the same composition as that for no. 4), Ni/C catalyst for the anode, and Pt/C catalyst for the cathode. A fuel cell was operated at 60 °C with 1 M KOH containing 10 wt% hydrazine as a fuel. The current density/voltage (I/V) and current density/power density (I/W) curves of the fuel cell are shown in Fig. 13a. The open circuit voltage (OCV) was 0.88 V both with oxygen and air. The high OCV values reflect the low hydrazine permeability of the QPAF-4 membrane. The ohmic resistance was nearly constant at 100 mΩ cm2 both with oxygen and air (Fig. 13b). Assuming that the hydroxide ion conduction of the membrane was most responsible for the ohmic resistance, the hydroxide ion conductivity was calculated to be ca. 50 mS cm−1, which is slightly lower than that of the QPAF-4 membrane (67 mS cm−1 with an IEC = 1.84 meq. g−1) at 60 °C in Fig. 6. The minor difference in the conductivity would be due to the contact resistance between the membrane and the catalyst layers. The fuel cell achieved high maximum power densities of 515 mW cm−2 (at a current density of 928 mA cm−2 with oxygen) and 323 mW cm−2 (at a current density of 696 mA cm−2 with air).
Fig. 13 (a) I/V and I/W curves and (b) the ohmic resistance of a hydrazine alkaline fuel cell using the QPAF-4 membrane operated at 60 °C (oxygen or air humidified at 62% relative humidity was supplied to the cathode).

The durability of the QPAF-4 membrane was evaluated in an operating fuel cell at a constant current density of 20 mA cm−2 (Fig. 14). The cell voltage was initially 0.77 V and decreased with operation time to 0.51 V after 1067 h. The average decay of the cell voltage was −244 μV h−1. The cell operation was discontinued from time to time to check the OCV value. The OCV, which is another measure for the membrane degradation, slightly decreased from 0.86 V (0 h) to 0.79 V (1067 h) with −66 μV h−1 as the average decay (note that the sudden jumps in the cell voltage represent the temporary stop of the operation). After the durability test, the recovered membrane did not show any mechanical failure with no detectable pinholes. The results suggest that the QPAF-4 membrane functioned well in the hydrazine alkaline fuel cell with reasonable stability.


Fig. 14 Durability of the cell voltage of the hydrazine alkaline fuel cell with the QPAF-4 membrane during long-term operation at 60 °C at a constant current density of 20 mA cm−2 (air humidified at 62% relative humidity was supplied to the cathode).

4. Conclusions

We have developed an advanced series of quaternized copolymers (QPAF-4) composed of perfluoroalkylene and fluorene functionalized with pendant hexyltrimethylammonium groups. The resulting copolymers did not contain any heteroatom linkages in the main chain. A simple two-step synthesis involving polymerization and quaternization provided the QPAF-4 copolymers with high molecular weights and controllable IECs. The copolymers were soluble in polar aprotic solvents and provided bendable and tough membranes by solution casting. The QPAF-4 membranes exhibited a volcano-type dependence of the hydroxide ion conductivity as a function of the IEC because of the large water absorbing properties of the high IEC membranes. Balanced conductivity and water uptake properties (86.2 mS cm−1 in water at 80 °C and 105% at r.t.) were obtained for the QPAF-4 membrane with IEC = 1.47 meq. g−1. The absence of heteroatom linkages in the main chain and the introduction of hexyl spacers for the pendant ammonium groups contributed to improving the alkaline stability. The QPAF-4 membranes retained hydroxide ion conductivity under harsh alkaline conditions in a 1 M KOH aqueous solution at 80 °C for 1000 h. Post-test analyses of the 1H NMR spectra, solubility, and mechanical properties revealed no, or negligible, structural and property changes. A hydrazine alkaline fuel cell using the QPAF-4 membrane was successfully operated with humidified oxygen or air. The maximum power density obtained was 515 mW cm−2 at a current density of 928 mA cm−2 with oxygen, proving that the QPAF-4 membrane functioned well in an operating fuel cell. The durability of the membrane was further confirmed in long-term operation (>1000 h) at a constant current density.
